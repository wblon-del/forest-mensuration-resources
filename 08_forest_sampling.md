# Module 8: Forest Sampling

## 8.1 Why sample?

Measuring every tree in a large forest is often impractical. Sampling allows estimates to be made from a representative subset of the population.

## 8.2 Common sampling designs

- Simple random sampling
- Systematic sampling
- Stratified sampling
- Cluster or two-stage sampling
- Variable-radius or angle-count sampling

## 8.3 Plot size

Plot size should be appropriate to forest type, stand structure, variability, objectives and available resources. Very small plots can produce unstable estimates in heterogeneous forests; very large plots increase field effort.

## 8.4 Systematic sampling

A regular grid or transect arrangement can provide good spatial coverage. The sampling interval should be selected from the required sample intensity and survey design.

## 8.5 Stratification

If the forest can be divided into relatively homogeneous strata, separate estimates can be made and combined using appropriate weights.

## 8.6 Basic expansion

For a fixed-area plot:

**Expansion factor = 1 / plot area (ha)**.

Example: a 0.05-ha plot has an expansion factor of 20.

## 8.7 Sampling error

Sampling estimates should be accompanied by an appropriate measure of uncertainty when the survey design supports it. Larger sample sizes generally reduce sampling error, but good design and representative coverage are equally important.
